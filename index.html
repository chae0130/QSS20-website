The Question

Measuring Generosity That Has No Official Answer

No single official measure captures how generous U.S. states are toward people with disabilities. The State Disability Policy Index fills that gap.

Assembled from 36 indicators scraped from federal and state government websites — verified by calling regional program officers directly — the SDPI covers SSI supplements, HCBS expenditure ratios, Section 811 housing, subminimum wage policy, and more.

36 indicators across 6 policy domains, each rescaled to 0–1 where 1 = most generous to people with disabilities

Three normalization strategies — min-max, z-score, and a hybrid — allow the ranking to be stress-tested. Toggle between them above the map.

Headline Finding

A Deep, Structural Regional Divide

The geographic pattern is striking — and consistent across all three methods. Northeastern and Pacific states cluster at the top. Southern states occupy the bottom, almost without exception.

1.4 SD gap between the Northeast mean (0.50) and the South mean (0.20) — a structural divide, not a few outliers

This reflects decades of divergent policy choices: cash transfers, Medicaid generosity, community-based service infrastructure, and pandemic-era reinvestment.

Key Insight

Community-Based Services Drive the Widest Differences

PCA reveals that disability policy is genuinely multidimensional — no single domain explains everything. But Home & Community-Based Services (HCBS) captures the most interstate variation.

0.86 vs. 0.07 Massachusetts vs. Missouri on HCBS — whether a person with a disability can live in their community or must enter an institution to receive care

HCBS includes workforce density of home health aides, presumptive eligibility, and the share of Medicaid long-term care spending delivered at home versus in nursing facilities.

Limitations & Next Steps

Policy on Paper ≠ Policy in Practice

The SDPI captures formal policy generosity — what the law authorizes, not what recipients experience. A state can have generous statutes while creating enrollment barriers that exclude most eligible people.

Future work should connect SDPI scores to actual outcomes — employment rates, institutionalization, poverty — to test whether high-scoring states produce better lives.

The index weights each domain equally. A Section 811 housing slot counts the same as a Medicaid eligibility expansion affecting orders of magnitude more people. Alternative weighting schemes are a natural extension.

Methodology

How the Index Was Built

The SDPI aggregates 36 policy indicators across six substantive domains, each normalized to a 0–1 generosity scale and verified against primary government sources. Three independent aggregation methods confirm the ranking is robust to methodological choices.
